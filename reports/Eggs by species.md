# Eggs by species

What each egg-laying species in the game really laid, or the best evidence for it, and a readable
look for a box-model egg about 10 px tall. Researched 2026-11-06.

How to read it: **Evidence** is a measured or published fact about that species or its family.
**Inference** follows from close relatives. **Guess** is a design pick where nothing is known,
chosen so families stay distinct. Real sizes run from 1 mm (fish) to 20 cm (big hadrosaurs).
In the game, shape, colour, texture and nest layout carry an egg's identity first. Size can be a
second cue in three tiers (small / medium / large).

## Dromaeosaurs: Velociraptor mongoliensis, V. osmolskae, Utahraptor ostrommaysi

No egg is known for any of these three. The nearest evidence is one dromaeosaur egg and a few
eggshells.

- **Size**: Guess, scaled from relatives. Velociraptor (15-20 kg) ~9 × 4.5 cm. Utahraptor
  (300-500 kg) ~18 × 8 cm. Evidence for scale: the Deinonychus egg's parent had a pelvic canal and
  shell thickness "very similar" to Citipati (18 cm eggs) (Grellet-Tinner & Makovicky 2006).
  Montanoolithus, a clutch from a dromaeosaurid or caenagnathid, has eggs ~12.5 × 6 cm
  (Zelenitsky & Therrien 2008).
- **Shape**: Inference: elongated (about 2:1), like all maniraptoran eggs. Exact shape unknown,
  because the Deinonychus egg is crushed. Design pick: one end slightly narrower, bird-like, so it
  reads differently from the Citipati egg, whose ends match.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, two calcite layers, 0.44 mm thick (up to 0.6 mm at bumps). The surface
  has "anastomosed bumps and ridges", a lumpy net (Grellet-Tinner & Makovicky 2006). A Korean
  eggshell with a similar net ornament, Reticuloolithus, is a possible dromaeosaurid shell
  (Choi et al. 2019). Montanoolithus also has a network of ridges.
- **Colour**: Evidence for Deinonychus: its eggshell held both pigments, blue-green biliverdin and
  red-brown protoporphyrin. Eumaniraptoran eggs were spotted and speckled (Wiemann, Yang & Norell
  2018). The museum's reconstruction shows a blue egg with brown spots. Applying this to
  Velociraptor and Utahraptor is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: an adult Deinonychus lay with its belly ribs on an egg, so it probably
  brooded, as oviraptorids did. Montanoolithus has at least 5 eggs in a ring on a sandstone mound,
  estimated at about 12 eggs in all. Inference from Troodon, a close relative: eggs stood nearly
  upright with only their lower parts in the sediment, and the adult sat on them (Varricchio et
  al. 2013).
- **Look in the game**: a medium oval, slightly narrower at the top, pale sky-blue/teal with brown
  speckles and a lumpy surface; 6-12 single eggs standing steep and half-buried in a small ring on
  a low mound. Utahraptor's egg is bigger, with more and larger spots.

## Citipati osmolskae (oviraptorid)

The best-known non-bird dinosaur nest. Citipati was found sitting on its eggs.

- **Size**: Evidence: 18 cm long, the largest eggs certainly from an oviraptorid (Clark, Norell &
  Chiappe 1999). Width about 6.5 cm (approx., from the family's ratio of 2.4-2.8).
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongatoolithid, "at least twice as long as wide". Long and narrow, both
  ends similar, slightly tapered.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard. Elongatoolithid eggs have lengthwise ridges and nodes that run in
  lines along the long axis, called linearituberculate (Macroolithus, Elongatoolithus).
- **Colour**: Evidence for a close relative: Heyuannia eggs were blue-green, with more biliverdin
  than protoporphyrin, so probably plain without spots (Wiemann et al. 2017). Oviraptorid eggs
  belong to the group whose eggs were coloured (Wiemann et al. 2018). Inference for Citipati:
  deep blue-green, few or no spots.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: about 22 eggs in a circular mound nest. The eggs lie in pairs, tilted,
  in concentric rings up to three layers deep, with their long axes running outward (Clark et al.
  1999; Varricchio 2000). Females formed and laid two eggs at a time: a pair of shelled eggs was
  found inside one female (Sato et al. 2005). The adult sat in the open centre with its arms spread
  over the rings.
- **Look in the game**: a very long, narrow, dark teal/blue-green egg with fine lengthwise ridges,
  laid in pairs in a ring around an empty centre where the adult sits.

## Protoceratops andrewsi and Psittacosaurus lujiatunensis (ceratopsians)

- **Size**: Evidence for Protoceratops: 10.07 × 5.81 cm, "elongated" (Erickson et al. 2017).
  Psittacosaurus: no egg is known, and the "34-egg clutch" in some lists is really 34 juveniles
  with an adult (Meng et al. 2004). Guess ~7 × 4 cm, scaled to a 20-30 kg animal.
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongated, about 1.7:1. The embryos lie curled inside an egg-shaped halo
  (Norell et al. 2020).
- **Shell**: Evidence: **soft and unmineralised**, like a turtle's or lizard's egg. The fossils show
  a dark organic film, not calcite. Hard-shelled dinosaur eggs evolved at least three times from a
  soft-shelled ancestor (Norell et al. 2020). For Psittacosaurus this is an inference from
  Protoceratops and the soft-shelled ornithischian ancestor.
- **Colour**: Unknown. Inference: no pigment. Ornithischian eggs tested show none (Wiemann et al.
  2018), and buried eggs gain nothing from colour. Design pick: pale buff/cream, darker where soil
  touches them.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: the Protoceratops clutch at Ukhaa Tolgod has at least 12 eggs. Soft eggs
  must be kept moist, so the authors infer the clutch was buried in sand or soil, perhaps warmed by
  rotting plants (Norell et al. 2020). For Psittacosaurus, a Lujiatun slab holds 24 hatchlings
  with one older animal. That could be a nest after hatching, but no eggshell was found (Hedrick
  et al. 2014).
- **Look in the game**: a slim, pale buff, soft egg with a slight dent or wrinkle (one off-shade
  pixel line) and no shine; a clutch of 12 or more crowded together, half-sunk in a sand pit, with
  no rings. Psittacosaurus has the same egg, smaller.

## Gastonia burgei and Pinacosaurus grangeri (ankylosaurs)

**No ankylosaur egg or eggshell has been identified.** Eggs of stegosaurs and ankylosaurids are
unknown (Moreno-Azanza et al. 2016). Everything below is a clearly labelled guess.

- **Size**: Guess ~12 × 10.5 cm, between Protoceratops and hadrosaur eggs for a 1-2 t animal.
- **Shape**: Guess: near-round (1.15:1), squat and wide like the animal.
- **Shell**: Guess: soft or leathery. The early ornithischian egg was soft (Norell et al. 2020),
  and no hard ankylosaur shell has turned up anywhere, even in rocks rich in other dinosaur
  eggshell. A missing hard shell is weak evidence for a soft one.
- **Colour**: Guess: no pigment (ornithischian, buried). Design pick: dull olive-grey with darker
  earth mottling, so it reads apart from the cream ceratopsian and the ivory ornithopod eggs.
- **Clutch**: Guess: about 15-20 buried in a pit. Related evidence: Pinacosaurus juveniles are often
  found in groups of the same age, huddled or facing one way. That suggests young of one clutch
  stayed together after hatching (Currie et al. 2011).
- **Look in the game**: a round, dull olive-grey, leathery egg with dark earth mottling and no
  ridges; a heap of them half-buried in a shallow pit.

## Iguanacolossus fortis (iguanodontian ornithopod)

No Iguanacolossus egg is known. The nearest evidence is the spheroolithid eggs of hadrosaurs and
eggshell probably from a related Early Cretaceous styracosternan.

- **Size**: Inference ~16 × 14 cm. Evidence for scale: Maiasaura eggs are about 900 mL (about a
  12 cm sphere), Hypacrosaurus eggs are 18.5 × 20 cm and about 3.9 L (Erickson et al. 2017; Xing et
  al. 2022), and Iguanacolossus is a large 9 m animal.
- **Shape**: Evidence (family): spherical to subspherical, a prolate spheroid (Spheroolithidae).
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard and two-layered. The surface is sagenotuberculate, a net of nodes and
  ridges with pits between them, or nearly smooth. Guegoolithus, eggshell from Spain's Early
  Cretaceous probably laid by a styracosternan ornithopod (Iguanacolossus's group), has the net
  ornament more strongly than Spheroolithus (Moreno-Azanza et al. 2014).
- **Colour**: Evidence: no pigment in Maiasaura eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). Design pick: ivory
  or bone-white.
- **Clutch**: Evidence from Maiasaura: about 30-40 eggs in a bowl dug into an earth mound about 2 m
  across, in circular layers and probably covered. Nests were spaced about 7 m apart in colonies
  (Horner & Makela 1979). A Chinese hadrosauroid clutch has at least 13 eggs (Xing et al. 2022).
- **Look in the game**: a big near-round ivory egg with a fine net of raised bumps (a checker of
  light and shade pixels); 20-30 in circles inside a low earth mound with plant litter on top.

## Cedarosaurus weiskopfae (brachiosaurid / titanosauriform sauropod)

No brachiosaurid egg is known. The nearest evidence is Megaloolithus eggs of titanosaurs, which
are close relatives within Titanosauriformes. Auca Mahuevo in Argentina has them with embryos.

- **Size**: Evidence (titanosaur): 12-14 cm across. Sauropod eggs were small for the animal's size,
  smaller than big hadrosaur eggs (Chiappe et al. 1998; Chiappe et al. 2003).
- **Shape**: Evidence: spherical to subspherical.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard and thick (roughly 1-2 mm at Auca Mahuevo, approx.). The surface is covered with
  round nodes visible to the naked eye, some merged into short chains. This is called
  compactituberculate. The nodes are the coarsest of any egg here.
- **Colour**: Evidence: no pigment in titanosaur eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). Design pick:
  chalk-white to pale grey, like the fossils.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: 15-40 eggs per clutch, usually 20-40, in a shallow dug pit, sometimes piled
  2-3 layers deep. Hundreds of clutches lie together in nesting grounds. The eggs were buried and
  then left (Chiappe et al. 1998, 2003; Grellet-Tinner & Fiorelli 2010).
- **Look in the game**: a perfectly round chalk-white egg covered in coarse round knobs (a
  1-pixel dot pattern); a heap of 20-40 piled in a shallow pit with no adult nearby.

## Crocodilians: Caiman yacare, Caiman crocodilus, and a Cretaceous neosuchian

- **Size**: Evidence. C. yacare 67.7 × 42.6 mm, 75 g (Pantanal, 49 nests; Papéis Avulsos de Zoologia study). C.
  crocodilus 63.8 × 40.7 mm (Venezuelan llanos, 323 eggs; Staton & Dixon 1977). Neosuchian:
  Krokolithes dinophilus, the oldest crocodylomorph eggs (Late Jurassic, Portugal), are about
  70 × 40 mm, within the range of living crocodilians (Russo et al. 2017).
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongate ellipsoid with both ends equally blunt, about 1.6:1 (1.75:1 for
  Krokolithes and the Early Cretaceous Glen Rose egg).
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, thin calcite (0.2-0.5 mm), and the outside is smooth with very few
  pores. During incubation it is etched and flakes off, showing the soft membrane underneath
  (Ferguson 1985; FAO crocodile guide).
- **Colour**: Evidence: white. No pigment in alligator eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). A live
  egg grows an **opaque chalk-white band** round its middle that spreads to both ends. Eggs from
  mound nests are often stained brown by the rotting plants. Fossil eggs: white is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: C. yacare averages 31 eggs (21-38). Mounds of plant litter and soil
  average 134 × 117 × 40 cm. C. crocodilus averages ~29 (11-38), also in vegetation mounds, sometimes
  sharing communal nesting areas. The eggs lie in layers in a chamber inside the mound, and the
  female guards it. Neosuchian: mound or hole nest is an inference from living crocodilians.
  A 13-egg crocodylomorph clutch, Suchoolithus, is known from the Late Jurassic (Russo et al. 2017).
- **Look in the game**: a small, smooth, white, capsule-shaped egg with a chalkier band round the
  middle and brown stains; 25-35 stacked inside a heap of leaves and mud with a caiman beside it.
  The neosuchian egg is the same, a little longer.

## Priscagama gobiensis (Cretaceous agamid lizard)

No Priscagama egg is known. The model is living agamid lizards.

- **Size**: Guess ~1.6 × 1 cm for a small lizard with a skull of a few centimetres. Evidence for
  scale: Blanford's rock agama lays eggs of 12.3-13.0 × 7.8-8.4 mm (Journal of Animal Diversity note).
- **Shape**: Evidence (agamids): oval, both ends similar.
- **Shell**: Evidence: soft, leathery parchment that dents and takes up water.
- **Colour**: Evidence (agamids): plain white. Applying this to Priscagama is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence (agamids): usually 4-10 eggs in a hole the female digs in damp sand or
  soil, then fills in. Agama agama digs about 5 cm deep.
- **Look in the game**: a tiny, plain white, matte oval with a small dent; 4-8 together in a
  sand hole, mostly hidden.

## Fish

In the game these are roe clusters, not single box eggs. Each kind should still read differently.

### Salmo trutta (brown trout)
- **Size**: Evidence: 4.6-6.1 mm, mean about 5.2 mm, 0.1 g. Females lay 1,500-3,500 eggs per kg of
  body weight (Bosnian hatchery study; Turkish hatchery data agree).
- **Shape and shell**: sphere, soft but firm, slightly sticky at first.
- **Colour**: Evidence: translucent orange to amber, from carotenoids. Later, two black eye spots
  show ("eyed ova").
- **Clutch**: Evidence: in autumn the female digs a redd, a pit in coarse gravel in flowing
  water, lays hundreds of eggs and covers them 1-20 cm deep. Redds are about 1-2 m long
  (Syrjänen et al., Finnish redd survey).
- **Look in the game**: a cluster of glossy orange-amber beads tucked between gravel stones;
  later beads show a black dot.

### Piaractus mesopotamicus (pacu)
- **Size**: Approx.: ripe oocytes about 0.5-1 mm. After spawning they take up water and swell to
  about 3 mm with a large clear space round the yolk. This value comes from related migratory
  characins; Brycon eggs are 1.5-3 mm hydrated. Hatching takes about 14-18 h at 27 °C.
- **Shape and shell**: sphere, soft, with a clear outer layer.
- **Colour**: Evidence: transparent, with a faint yellowish yolk.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: a migratory spawner that lays all its eggs at once at the start of the
  floods. Eggs are non-adhesive and semi-buoyant. They are scattered in open water and drift
  downstream into flooded plains. Thousands of eggs, no nest and no care.
- **Look in the game**: a loose cloud of tiny clear beads with yellow dots, drifting in water.

### Ceratodus kempae (extinct lungfish; Neoceratodus forsteri as model)
- **Size**: Evidence (Neoceratodus): egg 3-3.5 mm in a jelly capsule 2-3 mm thick, about 1 cm
  across in all (Kemp 1982).
- **Shape and shell**: a dark sphere inside a clear jelly ball. The jelly is sticky when fresh.
- **Colour**: Evidence: the yolk is green or brown with dark pigment granules, and the jelly is
  clear.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: laid singly or in pairs among dense water plants and roots, stuck to the
  leaves, in batches of 50-100 spread over plants in flowing water. The parents give no care.
  Applying this to the extinct species is an inference.
- **Look in the game**: single clear jelly balls, each with a dark green dot, stuck one by one
  along waterweed stems.

### Lycoptera davidi (Jurassic-Cretaceous osteoglossomorph; Hiodon as model)
- **Model**: its nearest living relatives are the mooneye and goldeye (Hiodon). Arapaima and
  arowanas build nests or brood eggs in the mouth, but they are larger and less closely related.
- **Size**: Evidence (goldeye): about 4 mm, spherical, semi-buoyant (Battle & Sprules 1960).
- **Colour**: Guess: pale straw, translucent.
- **Clutch**: Evidence (Hiodon): 5,000-20,000 eggs broadcast over gravel and pebbles in moving
  water in spring. The eggs drift near the bottom, with no nest and no care.
- **Look in the game**: a scatter of pale straw beads lying loose on pebbles in current. Unlike
  trout eggs, they are not buried and not orange.

## Wyvern (fantasy dragon)

Not a real animal, so there is no evidence. The design references are the qualities Ronald likes
in the Game of Thrones dragon eggs: **scaled surface, stone-like or petrified feel, jewel
colours, metallic sheen**. We design our own egg from those qualities and copy no show design: no
particular egg's colours, pattern or silhouette. Keep it the only scaled, shiny egg in the game,
so it cannot be mistaken for a real species.

- **Look in the game** (our pick): a large, slightly pointed egg covered in overlapping scale
  rows, deep jewel colour with a bright metallic rim on each scale, laid alone or in pairs on bare
  rock.

## Birds

The living birds are well measured, so most of what follows is evidence. The Cretaceous birds are
not: no egg is known for Confuciusornis, and the eggs once called "Gobipteryx eggs" turned out to
hold a different bird. Bird eggs differ from the reptile eggs above in two ways the game can use:
one end is usually narrower than the other, and many are coloured and marked.

### Common raven (Corvus corax)
- **Size**: Evidence: 4.4-5.2 × 3.1-3.6 cm (Cornell, All About Birds).
- **Shape**: Evidence: ordinary bird oval, one end blunter.
- **Shell**: hard, smooth, a little glossy.
- **Colour**: Evidence: "green, olive, or blue, often mottled with dark greenish, olive, or
  purplish brown" (Cornell); "pale bluish-green, brown-blotched" (Wikipedia, after Snow & Perrins).
  The ground colour varies between females.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: 3-7 eggs, usually 4-6, one brood a year. The nest is a deep bowl of large
  sticks in a big tree or on a cliff ledge, sometimes on a building or pylon, bound with roots, mud
  and bark and lined with fur or wool. The inner cup is 23-30 cm across and 13-15 cm deep, and nests
  are reused from year to year (Cornell; Wikipedia).
- **Look in the game**: a medium pointed oval, olive-green with bold dark brown-black blotches; 4-6
  in a fur-lined cup in a big stick bowl on a cliff ledge or treetop.

### House sparrow (Passer domesticus)
- **Size**: Evidence: 20-22 × 14-16 mm, about 2.9 g (Wikipedia, citing Cornell and BTO).
- **Shape**: Evidence: subelliptical, one end a little narrower.
- **Shell**: hard, thin, faint gloss.
- **Colour**: Evidence: white, bluish white or greenish white, spotted and blotched with grey or
  brown (Wikipedia, after Poole). The marks are usually dense, so the egg reads grey-brown overall.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 4-5 eggs (1-10 recorded), two or more broods a year. The nest is an
  untidy dome of stems and grass lined with feathers and scraps, about 20 × 30 cm, stuffed into a
  cavity: eaves, holes in walls, cliffs and banks, tree hollows; in warm places it is built in the
  open on a branch (Summers-Smith 1963, 1988, via Wikipedia).
- **Look in the game**: a tiny off-white egg so thickly freckled with grey-brown it looks dusty;
  4-5 in a messy grass-and-feather ball tucked into a hole or under a roof edge.

### Snow bunting (Plectrophenax nivalis)
- **Size**: Evidence: 2.0-2.6 × 1.5-1.8 cm (Cornell).
- **Shape**: Evidence: bird oval, one end narrower.
- **Shell**: hard, thin, faint gloss.
- **Colour**: Evidence, and sources differ: creamy white with variable brown spots and scrawls
  (Cornell); grey-white with reddish-brown speckles (Norwegian Polar Institute, Svalbard);
  blue-green with brown spots (Snow & Perrins 1998, via Wikipedia). The common thread is a pale
  ground with red-brown spots and scrawls.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 5-6 eggs (4-7; 2-9 recorded). A thick cup of grass, moss and
  rootlets lined with fur and feathers, often white feathers, set at the back of a rock crevice,
  under a slab or in scree, so it is rarely visible from outside (Cornell; Norwegian Polar
  Institute). Incubation 12-13 days.
- **Look in the game**: a tiny pale cream egg with red-brown spots and short scrawls, heaviest at
  the blunt end; 5-6 in a white-feather cup deep in a rock crack.

### Great horned owl (Bubo virginianus)
- **Size**: Evidence: mean 55 × 46.5 mm, 51 g (Los Angeles County birds, which are small; Turner,
  via Wikipedia). Cornell gives 5.3-5.6 × 4.5-4.7 cm.
- **Shape**: Evidence: nearly spherical, as owl eggs generally are.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, slightly rough, little gloss.
- **Colour**: Evidence: dull white, unmarked.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 2 eggs (1-4, very rarely 5-6). It builds no nest of its own: most
  tree nests are old stick nests of hawks, eagles, crows or ravens, with only a few feathers added,
  4.5-22 m up. It also lays in tree hollows, on cliff ledges and in small caves, and on the prairie
  on bare ground (Baumgartner 1938; Wikipedia). It is one of the earliest birds to nest, in late
  winter.
- **Look in the game**: a round, dull white, unmarked egg; 2 in a borrowed stick nest high in a
  tree, with a few grey feathers.

### Snowy owl (Bubo scandiacus)
- **Size**: Evidence: mean 56.4 × 44.7 mm (50-70.2 × 41-49.3 mm), about 53-60 g (Wikipedia, citing
  Holt et al. and others).
- **Shape**: Inference: near-round to short oval, a little longer than the great horned owl's
  egg, by the measurements.
- **Shell**: Evidence: smooth and glossy.
- **Colour**: Evidence: pure glossy white, unmarked.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: large and variable, usually 5-11, means 6.7-9.8 by region, up to 15-16.
  In years with few lemmings many pairs do not breed at all. The eggs are laid about 2 days apart
  and incubated from the first, so they hatch days apart and the chicks in one nest differ greatly
  in size. The nest is a bare round scrape the female digs in a dry, snow-free, windswept rise on
  open tundra, 25-48 cm across, with no material added (Wikipedia, citing Holt et al.).
- **Look in the game**: a round, glossy, pure white egg; 6-9 in a bare dent on top of a tundra
  hummock, with chicks of very different sizes once they hatch.

### Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos)
- **Size**: Evidence: mean 74.5 × 58 mm in North America (67.5-85.7 × 49.4-63.2; Bent 1937), 75 ×
  59 mm and about 145 g in Scotland (Watson 2010), via Wikipedia.
- **Shape**: Evidence: broad oval, one end slightly narrower.
- **Shell**: hard, thick, rough, little gloss.
- **Colour**: Evidence: from all white to white or cream with cinnamon, red-brown or grey-brown
  spots and blotches. The two eggs of one clutch often differ, one heavily marked and one pale
  (Britannica; Wikipedia).
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 2 eggs (1-4). The nest is a huge eyrie of heavy branches lined with
  grass or woodrush when in use, 1.3-1.8 m across and 0.5-1.1 m deep, growing with every year of use.
  A pair keeps 2-5 nests and uses them in turn. 95.6% of 410 Scottish nests were on cliffs; in
  some regions they are in the tallest trees (Watson 2010; Watson & Dennis 1992; Grubb & Eakle 1987;
  via Wikipedia).
- **Look in the game**: a large cream egg blotched with rusty red-brown over faint lilac-grey
  under-spots; 2 in a wide platform of thick sticks with a grass lining on a cliff ledge, one egg
  darker than the other.

### Savanna hawk (Buteogallus meridionalis)
- **Size**: Guess ~57 × 45 mm, scaled from the bird (approx. 0.8-1 kg) and from other raptors of
  that size. No measurement was found.
- **Shape**: Inference: broad oval, like other hawks.
- **Shell**: hard, little gloss (inference).
- **Colour**: Evidence: white (species accounts; Mader 1982). No markings are reported.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: always one egg. In the Venezuelan llanos all 116 clutches had a single egg,
  pairs raised at most one young a year, and they relaid after losing eggs. The nest is of sticks
  lined with grass, built in a palm tree (Mader 1982; Wikipedia).
- **Look in the game**: a plain white oval egg; 1 alone in a grass-lined stick nest in the top of
  a palm.

### Turkey vulture (Cathartes aura)
- **Size**: Evidence: 6.5-7.5 × 4.4-5.3 cm (Cornell, after Kirk & Mossman 1998).
- **Shape**: Inference: broad bird oval, one end blunter.
- **Shell**: hard, matte.
- **Colour**: Evidence: creamy white, sometimes tinged grey, blue or green, spotted and blotched
  with brown to purple, mostly around the large end; some eggs are nearly plain (Cornell, after Kirk
  & Mossman; older accounts mention lavender under-marks).
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 2 eggs (1-3). No nest at all: the eggs lie on the bare floor of a
  dark recess, such as a shallow cave, rock crevice, hollow log or tree, under a ledge or in an
  abandoned building.
- **Look in the game**: a large cream egg with brown-purple blotches crowded at the blunt end; 2 on
  the bare floor of a dark rock hollow, with no nest.

### Herring gull (Larus argentatus)
- **Size**: Evidence: means of 69-74 × 48-51 mm for first and second eggs in England, Denmark and
  Norway; the third egg is about 2 mm shorter and narrower (Vermeer 1971, table from Harris 1964,
  Paludan 1951 and Barth 1967).
- **Shape**: oval with a clearly narrower end, as gull eggs are.
- **Shell**: hard, matte, slightly rough.
- **Colour**: Evidence: usually olive-brown with dark brown to black speckles and blotches
  (Wikipedia). The ground runs from olive and green to buff between females, but a female's clutch
  usually matches. Colonies that eat more marine food lay more heavily spotted eggs
  (O'Hanlon et al. 2020).
- **Clutch**: Evidence: usually 3 eggs (2-4). Pairs nest in colonies, spaced as far
  apart as they can defend, on the ground on islands, on cliff ledges, and on roofs in towns
  (Animal Diversity Web; Wikipedia). The nest is a shallow cup of grass, seaweed and plant scraps
  on the ground (field-guide description; not checked against a cited source).
- **Look in the game**: a medium-large olive-brown egg with bold black-brown blotches; 3 in a
  grass cup on the ground, among many such nests on a rocky shore or cliff top.

### Gobipteryx minuta (enantiornithine, Late Cretaceous, Gobi)

No egg is securely Gobipteryx's. The best stand-in is **Gobioolithus minor**, small bird eggs from
the same Barun Goyot Formation. Elżanowski (1981) described late embryos inside them and compared
them with Gobipteryx minuta, so they were called "Gobipteryx eggs". Kurochkin, Chatterjee and
Mikhailov (2013) found the embryos belong to a different enantiornithine, Gobipipus reshetovi.
Gobipteryx is a similar small enantiornithine of the same rocks, so its egg was probably much the
same: inference.

- **Size**: Evidence (Gobioolithus minor): 30-46 × 20-24 mm, averaging about 43 × 21 mm at
  Khermeen Tsav (Mikhailov 1996; Varricchio & Barta 2015). The larger G. major is 50-53.5 ×
  25-32 mm.
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongated (length 1.8-2.0 times width, slimmer than most living birds' eggs)
  and slightly asymmetric, one pole narrower than the other.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, thin (0.1-0.2 mm), smooth, no ornament. Avian (ornithoid) structure in
  two or three layers.
- **Colour**: Unknown. Fossil eggs are pale cream, which is the rock's doing, not the bird's.
  Inference: the eggs may have been pigmented, because pigment was present in all eumaniraptoran
  eggs tested, the group that includes birds (Wiemann et al. 2018). Design pick: pale cream.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: at Khermeen Tsav the eggs lie singly, "irregularly dispersed over the
  slopes in vertical or sub vertical positions", on several levels that show a nesting ground used
  again and again (Mikhailov et al. 1994, quoted in Varricchio & Barta 2015). Sabath (1991) read
  their high porosity as a sign that they were buried, like a megapode's. The embryos have
  well-ossified skeletons with strong wings, so the chicks were probably precocial and active soon
  after hatching. Related evidence: a colony of enantiornithine eggs in Neuquén, Argentina, has 65
  eggs (41-47 × 26-29 mm, smooth) half-buried upright, **pointed end down**, about one egg-length
  apart, with no nest structure (Fernández et al. 2013).
- **Look in the game**: a small, slim, smooth, pale cream egg standing upright with its narrow end
  in the sand and its blunt top showing; many single eggs spaced across a sandy slope.

### Confuciusornis sanctus (early pygostylian, Early Cretaceous, Jehol)

**No Confuciusornis egg or eggshell is known.** What is known:

- Evidence: one specimen without long tail streamers has **medullary bone**, the calcium store
  only egg-laying female birds make, so Confuciusornis laid hard, calcified eggs (Chinsamy et al.
  2013).
- Disputed: 10-12 round bodies of 3.5-5.8 mm in the related Eoconfuciusornis were read as ovarian
  follicles (Zheng et al. 2017), as were similar ones in Jeholornis and an enantiornithine (Zheng et
  al. 2013), suggesting one working ovary as in living birds. Mayr et al. (2020) argue they are
  swallowed food; Bailleul et al. (2020) confirmed follicle tissue in one enantiornithine, but not
  in a confuciusornithid.
- Related evidence: an Early Cretaceous enantiornithine from China, Avimaia, died with one unlaid
  egg inside it. The shell has several layers, a sign the egg was held too long (egg-binding), and
  a single egg in the body fits one egg laid at a time (Bailleul et al. 2019).

Everything below is inference or guess.

- **Size**: Guess ~40 × 25 mm, for a crow-sized bird, in the range of the Cretaceous enantiornithine
  eggs above.
- **Shape**: Inference: elongated and asymmetric, as every known Mesozoic bird egg is.
- **Shell**: Inference: hard (medullary bone), thin and smooth, as in enantiornithine eggs.
- **Colour**: Unknown. Inference as for Gobipteryx: pigment was likely possible. Design pick: pale
  cream, a shade warmer than Gobipteryx.
- **Clutch**: Inference: troodontids (just outside birds) and enantiornithines (closer to living
  birds than Confuciusornis) both stood their eggs upright, half in the ground. That bracket makes the same
  habit likely for Confuciusornis (Varricchio & Barta 2015: "sediment-bound upright eggs appear
  common to basal birds"). Clutch size: guess, a few eggs. Its fossils come from lake beds, so a sandy
  lake shore is a fair setting.
- **Look in the game**: the Gobipteryx egg a little bigger and warmer cream; a few standing upright,
  narrow end down, in sand near water.

### Bird egg looks

Five looks cover the eleven birds. Each has one signature that no existing look has. Species in
a look share the egg; the nest around it tells them apart.

1. **Plain white bird egg** (great horned owl, snowy owl, savanna hawk). Near-round, pure white,
   **no marks at all**, one bright gloss pixel, medium size. Not the sauropod ball: smaller,
   smooth and glossy, with no knobs. Not the crocodilian capsule: rounder, no chalk band or
   stains. Not the lizard egg: several times bigger and glossy, not matte. It is the only unmarked
   bird egg. Nests: snowy owl 6-9 in a bare tundra scrape; great horned owl 2 in an old stick nest;
   savanna hawk 1 in a palm-top nest.
2. **Speckled songbird egg** (house sparrow, snow bunting). The **smallest bird egg**, pale ground
   **densely freckled** with grey-brown to red-brown, crowded at the blunt end. Not the lizard egg:
   marked, pointed at one end, faint gloss. Not the dromaeosaur egg: a fraction of the size, cream
   not sky-blue, smooth not lumpy, freckled rather than sparsely spotted. Not the olive look: tiny,
   and fine freckles rather than big blotches. Nests: a feather-lined cup hidden in a hole or
   crack, 4-6 eggs.
3. **Olive blotched egg** (raven, herring gull). Medium-large pointed oval, **olive-green to
   olive-brown with bold black-brown blotches** two or three pixels wide. Not the ankylosaur egg,
   the one real risk: that egg is round, dull grey-olive, matte and softly mottled, half-buried in a
   pit; this one is pointed, greener and lighter, with sharp dark blotches and a slight sheen, and
   sits in a nest above the ground. Not the dromaeosaur egg: olive, not sky-blue, and blotched,
   not speckled. Keep the raven on its olive-green colour, not its blue one, which would read as
   the dromaeosaur's. Nests: raven 4-6 in a big stick bowl on a ledge; gull 3 in a grass cup, many
   nests together.
4. **Rust-blotched cream egg** (golden eagle, turkey vulture). Large broad oval, **warm cream
   with rusty red-brown blotches over faint lilac under-spots**, heaviest at the blunt end, one egg
   of a pair paler. Not the ceratopsian egg: hard, marked and pointed at one end, not soft, plain and
   buried. Not the ornithopod egg: oval, no netted texture, and marked. Not the olive look: warm
   cream and rust against cool olive and black. Nests: eagle 2 on a huge stick platform on a
   cliff; vulture 2 on the bare floor of a dark hollow.
5. **Mesozoic bird egg** (Gobipteryx, Confuciusornis). Small, **slim (about 2:1), smooth, pale
   cream, always shown standing upright with the narrow end in the sand**, spaced singly about an
   egg-length apart. Not the dromaeosaur egg, which also stands steeply: smaller, smooth not
   lumpy, pale cream not blue with speckles, and scattered singly rather than in a ring under an
   adult. Not the oviraptorid egg: a third of the length, pale, smooth, single not paired. Not the
   ceratopsian egg: hard and glossy, upright and spaced rather than crowded and buried. Not the
   plain white bird egg: slim, cream, planted upright. Confuciusornis is the larger, warmer one.
