# Eggs by species

What each egg-laying species in the game really laid, or the best evidence for it, and a readable
look for a box-model egg about 10 px tall. Researched 2026-11-06.

How to read it: **Evidence** is a measured or published fact about that species or its family.
**Inference** follows from close relatives. **Guess** is a design pick where nothing is known,
chosen so families stay distinct. Real sizes run from 1 mm (fish) to 20 cm (big hadrosaurs).
In the game, shape, colour, texture and nest layout carry an egg's identity first. Size can be a
second cue in three tiers (small / medium / large).

## Dromaeosaurs: Velociraptor mongoliensis, V. osmolskae, Utahraptor ostrommaysi

No egg is known for any of these three. The nearest evidence is one dromaeosaur egg and a few
eggshells.

- **Size**: Guess, scaled from relatives. Velociraptor (15-20 kg) ~9 × 4.5 cm. Utahraptor
  (300-500 kg) ~18 × 8 cm. Evidence for scale: the Deinonychus egg's parent had a pelvic canal and
  shell thickness "very similar" to Citipati (18 cm eggs) (Grellet-Tinner & Makovicky 2006).
  Montanoolithus, a clutch from a dromaeosaurid or caenagnathid, has eggs ~12.5 × 6 cm
  (Zelenitsky & Therrien 2008).
- **Shape**: Inference: elongated (about 2:1), like all maniraptoran eggs. Exact shape unknown,
  because the Deinonychus egg is crushed. Design pick: one end slightly narrower, bird-like, so it
  reads differently from the Citipati egg, whose ends match.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, two calcite layers, 0.44 mm thick (up to 0.6 mm at bumps). The surface
  has "anastomosed bumps and ridges", a lumpy net (Grellet-Tinner & Makovicky 2006). A Korean
  eggshell with a similar net ornament, Reticuloolithus, is a possible dromaeosaurid shell
  (Choi et al. 2019). Montanoolithus also has a network of ridges.
- **Colour**: Evidence for Deinonychus: its eggshell held both pigments, blue-green biliverdin and
  red-brown protoporphyrin. Eumaniraptoran eggs were spotted and speckled (Wiemann, Yang & Norell
  2018). The museum's reconstruction shows a blue egg with brown spots. Applying this to
  Velociraptor and Utahraptor is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: an adult Deinonychus lay with its belly ribs on an egg, so it probably
  brooded, as oviraptorids did. Montanoolithus has at least 5 eggs in a ring on a sandstone mound,
  estimated at about 12 eggs in all. Inference from Troodon, a close relative: eggs stood nearly
  upright with only their lower parts in the sediment, and the adult sat on them (Varricchio et
  al. 2013).
- **Look in the game**: a medium oval, slightly narrower at the top, pale sky-blue/teal with brown
  speckles and a lumpy surface; 6-12 single eggs standing steep and half-buried in a small ring on
  a low mound. Utahraptor's egg is bigger, with more and larger spots.

## Citipati osmolskae (oviraptorid)

The best-known non-bird dinosaur nest. Citipati was found sitting on its eggs.

- **Size**: Evidence: 18 cm long, the largest eggs certainly from an oviraptorid (Clark, Norell &
  Chiappe 1999). Width about 6.5 cm (approx., from the family's ratio of 2.4-2.8).
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongatoolithid, "at least twice as long as wide". Long and narrow, both
  ends similar, slightly tapered.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard. Elongatoolithid eggs have lengthwise ridges and nodes that run in
  lines along the long axis, called linearituberculate (Macroolithus, Elongatoolithus).
- **Colour**: Evidence for a close relative: Heyuannia eggs were blue-green, with more biliverdin
  than protoporphyrin, so probably plain without spots (Wiemann et al. 2017). Oviraptorid eggs
  belong to the group whose eggs were coloured (Wiemann et al. 2018). Inference for Citipati:
  deep blue-green, few or no spots.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: about 22 eggs in a circular mound nest. The eggs lie in pairs, tilted,
  in concentric rings up to three layers deep, with their long axes running outward (Clark et al.
  1999; Varricchio 2000). Females formed and laid two eggs at a time: a pair of shelled eggs was
  found inside one female (Sato et al. 2005). The adult sat in the open centre with its arms spread
  over the rings.
- **Look in the game**: a very long, narrow, dark teal/blue-green egg with fine lengthwise ridges,
  laid in pairs in a ring around an empty centre where the adult sits.

## Protoceratops andrewsi and Psittacosaurus lujiatunensis (ceratopsians)

- **Size**: Evidence for Protoceratops: 10.07 × 5.81 cm, "elongated" (Erickson et al. 2017).
  Psittacosaurus: no egg is known, and the "34-egg clutch" in some lists is really 34 juveniles
  with an adult (Meng et al. 2004). Guess ~7 × 4 cm, scaled to a 20-30 kg animal.
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongated, about 1.7:1. The embryos lie curled inside an egg-shaped halo
  (Norell et al. 2020).
- **Shell**: Evidence: **soft and unmineralised**, like a turtle's or lizard's egg. The fossils show
  a dark organic film, not calcite. Hard-shelled dinosaur eggs evolved at least three times from a
  soft-shelled ancestor (Norell et al. 2020). For Psittacosaurus this is an inference from
  Protoceratops and the soft-shelled ornithischian ancestor.
- **Colour**: Unknown. Inference: no pigment. Ornithischian eggs tested show none (Wiemann et al.
  2018), and buried eggs gain nothing from colour. Design pick: pale buff/cream, darker where soil
  touches them.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: the Protoceratops clutch at Ukhaa Tolgod has at least 12 eggs. Soft eggs
  must be kept moist, so the authors infer the clutch was buried in sand or soil, perhaps warmed by
  rotting plants (Norell et al. 2020). For Psittacosaurus, a Lujiatun slab holds 24 hatchlings
  with one older animal. That could be a nest after hatching, but no eggshell was found (Hedrick
  et al. 2014).
- **Look in the game**: a slim, pale buff, soft egg with a slight dent or wrinkle (one off-shade
  pixel line) and no shine; a clutch of 12 or more crowded together, half-sunk in a sand pit, with
  no rings. Psittacosaurus has the same egg, smaller.

## Gastonia burgei and Pinacosaurus grangeri (ankylosaurs)

**No ankylosaur egg or eggshell has been identified.** Eggs of stegosaurs and ankylosaurids are
unknown (Moreno-Azanza et al. 2016). Everything below is a clearly labelled guess.

- **Size**: Guess ~12 × 10.5 cm, between Protoceratops and hadrosaur eggs for a 1-2 t animal.
- **Shape**: Guess: near-round (1.15:1), squat and wide like the animal.
- **Shell**: Guess: soft or leathery. The early ornithischian egg was soft (Norell et al. 2020),
  and no hard ankylosaur shell has turned up anywhere, even in rocks rich in other dinosaur
  eggshell. A missing hard shell is weak evidence for a soft one.
- **Colour**: Guess: no pigment (ornithischian, buried). Design pick: dull olive-grey with darker
  earth mottling, so it reads apart from the cream ceratopsian and the ivory ornithopod eggs.
- **Clutch**: Guess: about 15-20 buried in a pit. Related evidence: Pinacosaurus juveniles are often
  found in groups of the same age, huddled or facing one way. That suggests young of one clutch
  stayed together after hatching (Currie et al. 2011).
- **Look in the game**: a round, dull olive-grey, leathery egg with dark earth mottling and no
  ridges; a heap of them half-buried in a shallow pit.

## Iguanacolossus fortis (iguanodontian ornithopod)

No Iguanacolossus egg is known. The nearest evidence is the spheroolithid eggs of hadrosaurs and
eggshell probably from a related Early Cretaceous styracosternan.

- **Size**: Inference ~16 × 14 cm. Evidence for scale: Maiasaura eggs are about 900 mL (about a
  12 cm sphere), Hypacrosaurus eggs are 18.5 × 20 cm and about 3.9 L (Erickson et al. 2017; Xing et
  al. 2022), and Iguanacolossus is a large 9 m animal.
- **Shape**: Evidence (family): spherical to subspherical, a prolate spheroid (Spheroolithidae).
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard and two-layered. The surface is sagenotuberculate, a net of nodes and
  ridges with pits between them, or nearly smooth. Guegoolithus, eggshell from Spain's Early
  Cretaceous probably laid by a styracosternan ornithopod (Iguanacolossus's group), has the net
  ornament more strongly than Spheroolithus (Moreno-Azanza et al. 2014).
- **Colour**: Evidence: no pigment in Maiasaura eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). Design pick: ivory
  or bone-white.
- **Clutch**: Evidence from Maiasaura: about 30-40 eggs in a bowl dug into an earth mound about 2 m
  across, in circular layers and probably covered. Nests were spaced about 7 m apart in colonies
  (Horner & Makela 1979). A Chinese hadrosauroid clutch has at least 13 eggs (Xing et al. 2022).
- **Look in the game**: a big near-round ivory egg with a fine net of raised bumps (a checker of
  light and shade pixels); 20-30 in circles inside a low earth mound with plant litter on top.

## Cedarosaurus weiskopfae (brachiosaurid / titanosauriform sauropod)

No brachiosaurid egg is known. The nearest evidence is Megaloolithus eggs of titanosaurs, which
are close relatives within Titanosauriformes. Auca Mahuevo in Argentina has them with embryos.

- **Size**: Evidence (titanosaur): 12-14 cm across. Sauropod eggs were small for the animal's size,
  smaller than big hadrosaur eggs (Chiappe et al. 1998; Chiappe et al. 2003).
- **Shape**: Evidence: spherical to subspherical.
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard and thick (roughly 1-2 mm at Auca Mahuevo, approx.). The surface is covered with
  round nodes visible to the naked eye, some merged into short chains. This is called
  compactituberculate. The nodes are the coarsest of any egg here.
- **Colour**: Evidence: no pigment in titanosaur eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). Design pick:
  chalk-white to pale grey, like the fossils.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: 15-40 eggs per clutch, usually 20-40, in a shallow dug pit, sometimes piled
  2-3 layers deep. Hundreds of clutches lie together in nesting grounds. The eggs were buried and
  then left (Chiappe et al. 1998, 2003; Grellet-Tinner & Fiorelli 2010).
- **Look in the game**: a perfectly round chalk-white egg covered in coarse round knobs (a
  1-pixel dot pattern); a heap of 20-40 piled in a shallow pit with no adult nearby.

## Crocodilians: Caiman yacare, Caiman crocodilus, and a Cretaceous neosuchian

- **Size**: Evidence. C. yacare 67.7 × 42.6 mm, 75 g (Pantanal, 49 nests; Papéis Avulsos de Zoologia study). C.
  crocodilus 63.8 × 40.7 mm (Venezuelan llanos, 323 eggs; Staton & Dixon 1977). Neosuchian:
  Krokolithes dinophilus, the oldest crocodylomorph eggs (Late Jurassic, Portugal), are about
  70 × 40 mm, within the range of living crocodilians (Russo et al. 2017).
- **Shape**: Evidence: elongate ellipsoid with both ends equally blunt, about 1.6:1 (1.75:1 for
  Krokolithes and the Early Cretaceous Glen Rose egg).
- **Shell**: Evidence: hard, thin calcite (0.2-0.5 mm), and the outside is smooth with very few
  pores. During incubation it is etched and flakes off, showing the soft membrane underneath
  (Ferguson 1985; FAO crocodile guide).
- **Colour**: Evidence: white. No pigment in alligator eggshell (Wiemann et al. 2018). A live
  egg grows an **opaque chalk-white band** round its middle that spreads to both ends. Eggs from
  mound nests are often stained brown by the rotting plants. Fossil eggs: white is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: C. yacare averages 31 eggs (21-38). Mounds of plant litter and soil
  average 134 × 117 × 40 cm. C. crocodilus averages ~29 (11-38), also in vegetation mounds, sometimes
  sharing communal nesting areas. The eggs lie in layers in a chamber inside the mound, and the
  female guards it. Neosuchian: mound or hole nest is an inference from living crocodilians.
  A 13-egg crocodylomorph clutch, Suchoolithus, is known from the Late Jurassic (Russo et al. 2017).
- **Look in the game**: a small, smooth, white, capsule-shaped egg with a chalkier band round the
  middle and brown stains; 25-35 stacked inside a heap of leaves and mud with a caiman beside it.
  The neosuchian egg is the same, a little longer.

## Priscagama gobiensis (Cretaceous agamid lizard)

No Priscagama egg is known. The model is living agamid lizards.

- **Size**: Guess ~1.6 × 1 cm for a small lizard with a skull of a few centimetres. Evidence for
  scale: Blanford's rock agama lays eggs of 12.3-13.0 × 7.8-8.4 mm (Journal of Animal Diversity note).
- **Shape**: Evidence (agamids): oval, both ends similar.
- **Shell**: Evidence: soft, leathery parchment that dents and takes up water.
- **Colour**: Evidence (agamids): plain white. Applying this to Priscagama is an inference.
- **Clutch**: Evidence (agamids): usually 4-10 eggs in a hole the female digs in damp sand or
  soil, then fills in. Agama agama digs about 5 cm deep.
- **Look in the game**: a tiny, plain white, matte oval with a small dent; 4-8 together in a
  sand hole, mostly hidden.

## Fish

In the game these are roe clusters, not single box eggs. Each kind should still read differently.

### Salmo trutta (brown trout)
- **Size**: Evidence: 4.6-6.1 mm, mean about 5.2 mm, 0.1 g. Females lay 1,500-3,500 eggs per kg of
  body weight (Bosnian hatchery study; Turkish hatchery data agree).
- **Shape and shell**: sphere, soft but firm, slightly sticky at first.
- **Colour**: Evidence: translucent orange to amber, from carotenoids. Later, two black eye spots
  show ("eyed ova").
- **Clutch**: Evidence: in autumn the female digs a redd, a pit in coarse gravel in flowing
  water, lays hundreds of eggs and covers them 1-20 cm deep. Redds are about 1-2 m long
  (Syrjänen et al., Finnish redd survey).
- **Look in the game**: a cluster of glossy orange-amber beads tucked between gravel stones;
  later beads show a black dot.

### Piaractus mesopotamicus (pacu)
- **Size**: Approx.: ripe oocytes about 0.5-1 mm. After spawning they take up water and swell to
  about 3 mm with a large clear space round the yolk. This value comes from related migratory
  characins; Brycon eggs are 1.5-3 mm hydrated. Hatching takes about 14-18 h at 27 °C.
- **Shape and shell**: sphere, soft, with a clear outer layer.
- **Colour**: Evidence: transparent, with a faint yellowish yolk.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: a migratory spawner that lays all its eggs at once at the start of the
  floods. Eggs are non-adhesive and semi-buoyant. They are scattered in open water and drift
  downstream into flooded plains. Thousands of eggs, no nest and no care.
- **Look in the game**: a loose cloud of tiny clear beads with yellow dots, drifting in water.

### Ceratodus kempae (extinct lungfish; Neoceratodus forsteri as model)
- **Size**: Evidence (Neoceratodus): egg 3-3.5 mm in a jelly capsule 2-3 mm thick, about 1 cm
  across in all (Kemp 1982).
- **Shape and shell**: a dark sphere inside a clear jelly ball. The jelly is sticky when fresh.
- **Colour**: Evidence: the yolk is green or brown with dark pigment granules, and the jelly is
  clear.
- **Clutch**: Evidence: laid singly or in pairs among dense water plants and roots, stuck to the
  leaves, in batches of 50-100 spread over plants in flowing water. The parents give no care.
  Applying this to the extinct species is an inference.
- **Look in the game**: single clear jelly balls, each with a dark green dot, stuck one by one
  along waterweed stems.

### Lycoptera davidi (Jurassic-Cretaceous osteoglossomorph; Hiodon as model)
- **Model**: its nearest living relatives are the mooneye and goldeye (Hiodon). Arapaima and
  arowanas build nests or brood eggs in the mouth, but they are larger and less closely related.
- **Size**: Evidence (goldeye): about 4 mm, spherical, semi-buoyant (Battle & Sprules 1960).
- **Colour**: Guess: pale straw, translucent.
- **Clutch**: Evidence (Hiodon): 5,000-20,000 eggs broadcast over gravel and pebbles in moving
  water in spring. The eggs drift near the bottom, with no nest and no care.
- **Look in the game**: a scatter of pale straw beads lying loose on pebbles in current. Unlike
  trout eggs, they are not buried and not orange.

## Wyvern (fantasy dragon)

Not a real animal, so there is no evidence. The design references are the qualities Ronald likes
in the Game of Thrones dragon eggs: **scaled surface, stone-like or petrified feel, jewel
colours, metallic sheen**. We design our own egg from those qualities and copy no show design: no
particular egg's colours, pattern or silhouette. Keep it the only scaled, shiny egg in the game,
so it cannot be mistaken for a real species.

- **Look in the game** (our pick): a large, slightly pointed egg covered in overlapping scale
  rows, deep jewel colour with a bright metallic rim on each scale, laid alone or in pairs on bare
  rock.
