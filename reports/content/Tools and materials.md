# Tools and materials: real technology for the tier ladder

Research for the content plan. It covers what people really made tools and weapons from before
metal, how well those tools worked next to steel, the metals in the order people learned them,
the other fired and burnt materials (pottery, brick, lime, glass, tar), and where each raw
material really is in the places our biomes stand for. Section F is my proposal for a tier
ladder built on all this. 2026-11-03.

**How sure is it.** Numbers with a source carry its link [n]. Numbers marked *est.* are my
estimate or common craft knowledge. Experimental archaeology rarely gives clean numbers. Most
figures come from one or a few experiments, and runs of the same experiment differ by 2-5×
(felling times, bloom yields). Metal hardness depends more on how the piece was hammered than on
the alloy. Tool masses are mostly *est.*. For the Cretaceous there are no people, so that part is
geology only. Several primary papers (Mathieu & Meyer 1997, Jørgensen's Draved report) were
behind paywalls; their figures here come from abstracts and the summaries that cite them.

### A3. Stone against metal: felling trees

| Study | Tools | Trees | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saraydar & Shimada 1971 | ground stone vs steel | mixed | stone took over 6× as long and over 5× the energy | [1] |
| New Guinea, observed use | stone vs steel | 10-30 cm hardwood | stone: 10 cm in about 4 min, 30 cm in about 20 min; softwood about 30% slower; stone 2-2.5× slower than steel on small trees, 4-4.5× on large | [2] |
| Mathieu & Meyer 1997 (Vermont) | Neolithic stone, replica bronze, steel | 9 × 9 m plots | metal much faster than stone, more so on large trees; under about 15 cm little difference, above it metal up to 5× faster; bronze vs steel not statistically different | [3][4] |
| Flint-axe trials quoted by Britannica (not named; likely the Danish 1950s trials) | flint axes | oak, pine | after practice: oak over 30 cm in half an hour, pine 61 cm in under 20 min | [5] |
| Same trials, first days | flint axes | beech | four axes and their handles broke within five minutes when swung like steel axes; the right stroke is short and chipping | [2] |
| Chalain (Neolithic lake village replicas) | sleeve-hafted stone axes | 20 cm trunks | willow 30-60 min; ash 60-90 min | [6] |
| Forests 2023 | stone vs steel | 3.5-5.6 cm saplings | stone needed about 75 more strokes per tree; bending the stem first cut strokes by 73% | [7] |

What this means for the game:

- **Stone works.** A stone axe fells a small tree almost as fast as steel. The gap grows with the
  trunk: 2× on thin trees, 4-6× on thick ones.
- **Bronze is about as good as steel for felling** [3][4]. The big jump is stone to metal, not
  bronze to iron.
- **Technique matters as much as material.** Stone axes break when swung like steel ones.

Suggested felling-speed multipliers (steel = 1.0), *est.* from the table: stone 0.25-0.5
(falls with trunk size), copper 0.6, bronze 0.85, wrought iron 0.85, steel 1.0.

### A4. Edges and resharpening

| Material | Edge | Durability | Resharpening | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Obsidian | the finest edge of any material; widely quoted as about 3 nm, finer than a steel scalpel | brittle; chips on bone and wood | often; a fresh flake is quicker than retouch *est.* | widely repeated, not checked against a primary source |
| Flint, chert | very sharp | the toughest knappable stone in cutting tests *est.* | retouch a knife edge every few hundred cuts; reknap or regrind an axe after a day's heavy work *est.* | [9] for the retoucher |
| Quartzite, basalt | coarser edge | tough, does not chip as easily | grinding | *est.* |
| Ground stone axe | blunt angle, tough | can fell more than one tree per day; fails by chipping or haft breaking | regrind on sandstone | [2][5] |
| Copper | soft unless hammered | bends and dulls fast | re-hammer cold, then hone; can be fixed endlessly | [25] |
| Bronze | hammered edge hard | far tougher than stone, holds an edge | hone; recast when broken | [3][32] |

The game can model stone as **sharp but brittle** (high damage, short life, cheap to replace) and
metal as **tough and repairable** (an anvil restores durability).

is the wood-biome weapon** (yew and elm in the forest, Osage orange on the plains). The **sling
needs no wood** and fits the desert, beach and llanos. The **toggling harpoon** belongs to the
tundra and sea.

## C. Metals, in the order people learned them

### C1. The steps

| Step | Raw material | Process | Temperature | Needs | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Native copper | copper nuggets in streams and glacial gravels | cold-hammer with a stone; heat and quench to soften (anneal) when it gets brittle | an open fire for annealing (about 400-700 °C *est.*) | hammerstone, fire | [25][26] |
| | Ahtna of the Copper River cold-hammered Chitina nuggets with river cobbles; awls near Gulkana date to AD 900-1200 | | | | [26] |
| Smelted copper | malachite, azurite (green and blue oxide ores) with charcoal | crucible or bowl furnace under charcoal, forced air by blowpipes or bellows | malachite reduces at about 800 °C, but the copper only pools above 1,000 °C [27]; a blowpipe crucible fire reached 1,300 °C in 15 min [28]; lab smelts run to about 1,250 °C [29]; copper melts at 1,085 °C | clay crucible, blowpipes or bellows, charcoal | [27][28][29] |
| Arsenical copper | copper ores with arsenic (fahlore, or arsenic minerals added) | smelt as copper | as copper | as copper | [30] |
| Tin bronze | copper + tin (cassiterite) | melt and alloy, cast in stone or clay moulds, hammer the edge | bronze melts at about 950 °C | crucible, moulds, a furnace with bellows | [31] |
| Bloomery iron | bog ore or rock ore (hematite, limonite) roasted, with charcoal | clay shaft furnace, air from bellows for 3-7 h; the iron never melts; slag drains, a spongy bloom is left | 1,100-1,400 °C in the furnace; iron melts at about 1,540 °C; the slag is near fayalite and melts near 1,178 °C | clay furnace, bellows, a lot of charcoal, anvil, hammer, tongs | [35][36] |
| Steel | bloom iron + carbon | carbon from charcoal in the bloomery or by heating iron in charcoal (carburising); then quench and temper | quench from about 850 °C | forge, water trough | [35][37] |
| Gold | native gold in stream gravels (placers) and quartz veins | panning; hammered cold; melts at 1,064 °C | - | pan, sluice | *est.* |
| Silver, lead | galena (lead sulphide, often with silver) | smelt to lead, then cupel to pull the silver out | *est.* | furnace, cupel | [41] |

### C4. Hardness

Vickers hardness (HV). Higher is harder. Stone is harder than any of these metals but brittle.

| Material | State | HV | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | cast or annealed | about 40-50 *est.* | *est.* |
| Copper | cold-hammered | about 100-130 *est.* | *est.* |

| Tin bronze | annealed | 75-100 | [32] |

| Bronze (general range) | - | 60-258 | [31] |
| Wrought iron | ferrite | 80-120 (Wikipedia gives 30-80) | [40][31] |
| Steel | quenched (martensite) | 600-800, brittle | [40] |
| Steel | quenched and tempered | 300-500 *est.* | *est.* |
| Gold, silver | pure | about 25-30 *est.* | *est.* |
| Flint (quartz) | - | about 1,000-1,200 *est.* | *est.* |

What this means for the game:

- **Hammered bronze is harder than wrought iron.** Iron did not win on hardness. It won because
  its ore is almost everywhere (bog ore) while tin is rare, and because iron can become steel.
- **Steel is the only metal clearly better than bronze**, and only after quench and temper.
- **Copper is a short step.** Unhammered it is softer than everything; hammered it is about
  as good as soft bronze. It is worth having because native copper needs no furnace.

pit) → kiln (pottery, brick, lime, crucibles) → furnace with bellows (copper, bronze) → bloomery
(iron) → forge (steel). Glass sits late, after the kiln, and wants wood ash, so it fits the
forests.

Things that stand out:

- **Chert or flint is easy on the plains (Konza)** and hard to get in the forest (Kassel). Kassel
  has **basalt** for ground axes instead.
- **Obsidian** is real in three biomes: desert (Sonora), ocean (Terceira) and, far off, the taiga.
- **Copper** is native in the taiga's wider region (Copper River) and oxide ore in the desert
  (Sonora). The beach (Iberian Pyrite Belt) has copper too.
- **Tin is the rare one.** Of our places only Faro's region (Neves-Corvo) has it. Real Bronze
  Age Europe got it from Cornwall, Iberia and the Erzgebirge.
- **Bog iron** fits the forest (Białowieża), birch forest and taiga. **Rock iron** is huge at
  the swamp's edge (Urucum).
- **Gold** is placer gold in the taiga (Fairbanks) and desert (Sonora). **Silver and lead** are
  in the mountains (Davos).
- **No stone at all** on the tundra, llanos and Pantanal. Those peoples used bone, ivory, shell,
  wood and clay, and traded for stone. That is a good gameplay pressure.

### E2. The Cretaceous dimension

No people, so its materials are rock, wood, resin and animal parts.

| Biome | Formation | Rocks | Useful for crafting | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red dunes | Djadokhta Fm, Gobi | cemented red-orange and pale sandstone; less siltstone, mudstone, conglomerate; **caliche** beds; wind-blown dunes | sandstone; caliche as a lime source | [70] |
| Scrubland | Bayan Mandahu Fm, Inner Mongolia | red-bed sandstone, mostly structureless, laid by sandstorms | sandstone | [71] |
| Floodplain | Cedar Mountain Fm, Utah | conglomerate, sandstone, mudstone; the basal Buckhorn conglomerate is up to half **chert** pebbles; maroon mudstone with carbonate nodules; pedogenic calcrete and silcrete; nodular limestone | **chert** (knappable), limestone nodules | [72] |
| Fern forest | Yixian Fm, Liaoning | **basalt** and andesite lavas; shale and **tuff** (230-420 m); upper conglomerate and tuffaceous sandstone | basalt, fine shale, volcanic ash | [73] |
| Crags | Sevier highlands | thrust sheets of Paleozoic **quartzite** (Eureka quartzite), **limestone and dolomite**, Cambrian shale | quartzite, limestone | [74] |

Notes:

- **Djadokhta and Bayan Mandahu are Late Cretaceous** (Campanian, about 75-71 million years),
  not Early. Yixian (126-124 million years) and Cedar Mountain are Early Cretaceous [71][73].
  The dimension mixes the two; worth knowing if the plan claims "Early Cretaceous" throughout.
- **Amber**: my sources do not report amber from any of these five formations. Real Early
  Cretaceous amber comes from Lebanon (Barremian, from kauri-pine forests) and Spain (Álava,
  Albian, an *Agathis*-like resin) [75][76]. If the game wants amber, put it rare in the fern
  forest (wet conifer woods) *est.*.
- **Animal parts** are the dimension's real riches: bone, teeth and claws (points, awls), hide,
  sinew, scales, eggshell, feathers (Yixian's feathered dinosaurs) *est.*.
- **Wood**: conifers (araucarians, cheirolepids), cycads, ginkgo, tree ferns *est.*.

## F. A suggested tier ladder (my proposal)

Everything in this section is my proposal, built on sections A-E.

### F1. The tiers

| # | Tier | Tools and weapons | What it needs | Pushes the player toward | Real basis |

### F2. Numbers for the tiers (*est.*)

Durability is relative to the game's current iron tool (= 1.0). Felling speed is relative to
steel (= 1.0), from A3. Hardness rank comes from C4.

| Tier | Durability | Felling speed | Mining | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | 0.2 | - | dirt, sand | none |
| Knapped stone | 0.3 (sharp, brittle) | 0.25-0.5 | stone, coal | retouch with a flaker, cheap |
| Ground stone | 0.5 | 0.4-0.6 | stone, coal | regrind on sandstone |
| Copper | 0.6 | 0.6 | + copper and tin ore | re-hammer at an anvil, free |
| Bronze | 1.0 | 0.85 | + iron ore | hone; recast broken tools |
| Wrought iron | 1.1 (softer edge than bronze, tougher body) | 0.85 | + gold, silver ore | forge |
| Steel | 2.0 | 1.0 | everything | forge |

### F3. Design notes

- **Iron's advantage is supply, not hardness.** Make bronze good but hold it behind tin, which is
  rare and far away. Iron is a little worse than good bronze until it becomes steel, but its ore
  is in every wet forest.
- **Charcoal is the real cost of metal.** A bloomery run should eat a stack of charcoal, which
  eats a small forest. That ties the metal ages to the forest biomes and to tree regrowth.
- **The stoneless biomes** (tundra, llanos, Pantanal) should push bone, ivory, shell and trade.
  The Iñupiat show that a full kit (harpoon, bow, slat armour, ivory tools) needs no local stone.
- **Diamond and redstone have no real counterpart here.** If the game keeps a top tier,
  hardened steel (pattern-welded or crucible) is the real one. Obsidian can stand in as a
  special edge: the sharpest blade, the shortest life.
- **Armour** follows the same order *est.*: hide and rawhide → bone, wood or ivory slat armour
  (Arctic) → bronze plates → iron mail → steel plate.
