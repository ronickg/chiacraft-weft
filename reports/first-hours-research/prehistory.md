# The first hours with nothing: what early humans did, and what a person can really do

Research for chiacraft's "earned" primitive start. Each fact has a link after it. Where a number
comes from a hobbyist, a press story or a forum (not a study), the line says so. Collected
November 2026.

Short version: with bare hands you can pick things up and break things. A sharp flake takes
seconds. Everything after that (a haft, a cord, a fire, a ground axe) costs much more time, and
the time is often waiting (drying, retting, grinding), not swinging.

---

## 1. Knapping: making the first sharp stone

**The oldest tools are hard-hammer percussion: a hand-held hammerstone hits a core, and a sharp
flake comes off.**
- Oldest accepted stone tools (Oldowan): about 2.6 million years old, at Gona and Ledi-Geraru,
  Ethiopia. Older ones at Lomekwi 3, Kenya (about 3.3 Ma) are still argued over.
  [Cosmos](https://cosmosmagazine.com/archaeology/oldest-stone-tools-ever-found),
  [Turkana Basin Institute](https://turkanabasin.org/?p=34449)
- The first hand axes (Acheulean) are about 1.76 Ma old, at Kokiselei 4, Kenya. Oldowan tools went
  on being made beside them. [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Oldowan_Tools/.md)
- Battered cobbles (hammerstones) show that by 2.6 Ma people used one tool to make another.
  Experiments show that plain sharp flakes, not shaped "choppers", were the main butchery knife:
  skinning, cutting joints, taking meat off. Most cut marks on Oldowan bones are sets of parallel
  lines from flake edges.
  [Schick & Toth, Stone Age Institute (PDF)](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/pdfs/oldowan-ch1-schick-toth.pdf)
- At Olduvai, most tool stone came from less than 2 km away, and nearly all from within 4 km.
  Lava came as rounded stream cobbles. A few pieces came from 8-10 km away.
  [Schick & Toth (PDF)](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/pdfs/oldowan-ch1-schick-toth.pdf)

**Which stones work.** The stone has to break with a smooth, shell-shaped (conchoidal) fracture.
That means fine-grained, glassy, high-silica stone.
- Good: flint, chert, obsidian, jasper, chalcedony, fine quartzite, fine-grained basalt and other
  lavas (rhyolite, felsite).
  [Wikipedia: Knapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knapping)
- Hand axes are mostly flint, but rhyolite, phonolite and quartzite were used too. Obsidian was
  rarely used for hand axes because it shatters easily.
  [Wikipedia: Hand axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_axe)
- The early Oldowan used whatever was local: lavas (trachyte, basalt), quartz, quartzite, chert.
  Over time at Olduvai, quartz/quartzite took over from lava.
  [Schick & Toth (PDF)](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/pdfs/oldowan-ch1-schick-toth.pdf)
- Hard, tough cobbles such as quartzite make good hammerstones.
  [Wikipedia: Knapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knapping)
- Poor: coarse or uneven rock (granite, sandstone, limestone, common coarse basalt). It crumbles
  or breaks unpredictably. Coarse sandstone is instead used as a grinding slab.
  [Wikipedia: Knapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knapping),
  [Stone Tools Museum, Dickson axe](https://stonetoolsmuseum.com/artefact/australia/modern-art-f-p-dickson/6289/)

**How long it takes.**
- A flake: one strike can be enough. In a timed experiment, novices freehand knapping got
  **0.38 usable flakes per strike** (about one usable flake every 2-3 blows). Throwing a cobble
  against a rock got **1.2 usable flakes per strike**, and smashing a cobble on an anvil (bipolar)
  used up a core fastest. Novice knapping still gave the best butchery flakes.
  [Putt 2015 poster (PDF)](https://paleoanthro.net/media/meetings/files/Putt_2015.pdf)
- Kanzi, a bonobo, learned to make cutting flakes. He also found his own way: throwing a cobble at
  a hard floor. His flakes were smaller, and he failed more often than early humans did.
  [Stone Age Institute: Kanzi studies](https://stoneageinstitute.org/stonetoolapes.html),
  [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/kanzi-the-bonobo-who-learned-language-and-made-stone-tools-dies-at-age-44/)
- A chopper: a few blows. No study found that times one; replication papers note that chopper
  knapping needs little planning per strike, because a bad strike costs little (unverified
  snippet, so treat as our estimate: well under a minute).
- A hand axe, made by an expert: **under 15 minutes** for a good one; a rough one from a beach
  pebble in **under 3 minutes**. [Wikipedia: Hand axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_axe).
  Archaeologist David Miles puts it at about 20 minutes (forum report of his book *The Tale of
  the Axe*). [GreaterWrong comment](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/bkjqfhKd8ZWHK9XqF/should-you-make-stone-tools/comment/TsYzcSL3oFfPtnqzJ)
- A hand axe, made by a novice: in practice, not at all. In a training study, 26 novices trained
  for up to **90 hours** over months. Their accuracy improved, but only the experts made hand axes
  like real Late Acheulean ones.
  [Emory Anthropology: Pargeter et al. 2019](https://emoryanthropology.org/2019/07/15/dr-justin-pargeter-and-colleagues-present-a-multidisciplinary-study-of-late-acheulean-handaxe-making-skill-acquisition-in-the-latest-edition-of-the-journal-of-human-evolution/)
- 2 hours of training is too short to measure a novice getting better at flaking cores; direct
  teaching made novices more efficient and cut their errors.
  [Wilson, Stout et al. 2023, Lithic Technology](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/01977261.2023.2178767)

**Injuries and failures.**
- A 2020 survey of **173** modern knappers: about half get hurt often; **23%** had needed a
  doctor; the usual injuries are cuts and bruises on fingers and hands, and flakes in the eye.
  **35** said a flake had hit an eye. 86% now wear eye protection.
  [Wikipedia: Knapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knapping),
  [Kent State University](https://www.kent.edu/node/981743)
- Long-term flint dust causes silicosis ("knappers' rot"). Brandon gunflint knappers died at 44 on
  average, against 66 for other local men. [Wikipedia: Knapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knapping)
- Novices waste a lot: most strikes give no usable flake (see 0.38 above), and cores fail by
  step fractures and crushing.

## 2. Using unhafted stone, and how stone axes compare with steel

- Hand-held flakes cut meat, hide and soft plants very well. Hand axes were mainly cutting tools;
  at Boxgrove they were used almost only for butchery. Microwear on Peninj hand axes shows
  woodworking. [Wikipedia: Hand axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_axe)
- No good study times felling a tree with an unhafted hand axe. Hand-held, a hand axe is for
  cutting, scraping and trimming branches, not felling. John Plant's first stone tool in a new area
  was a hand axe from a creek stone, used to cut a staff and clear a path, not trees.
  [Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/2017/11/24/new-area-starting-from-scratch/)

**Hafted stone axes felling trees (all hafted, mostly polished Neolithic axes):**
- Laurel oak saplings, **3.5-5.6 cm** across: a stone axe needed on average **75 more strokes**
  than a steel axe. A helper bending the sapling cut stone-axe strokes by **123 (73%)** and steel
  by **15 (72%)**. That implies about **170 strokes with stone against about 20 with steel**
  (our arithmetic), so roughly **8x** on unbent saplings. Women and men felled equally well.
  [Putz et al. 2023, Forests (USC repository)](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Tree-Felling-with-Stone-Axes-Pre-Bending/99716034302621)
- With a polished stone axe in a sleeve haft: a **20 cm willow in 30-60 min**; a **20 cm ash in
  1-1.5 h** (about twice as long; hardwood matters).
  [French Ministry of Culture, Palafittes](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/palafittes/en/felling-trees)
- Danish tests with 4,000-year-old polished axes: once in practice, men felled an oak over **30 cm
  in about half an hour**, and a pine **61 cm in under 20 minutes**.
  [Britannica: Neolithic tools](https://www.britannica.com/technology/hand-tool/Neolithic-tools)
- Draved forest, Denmark (1952-54): three men with stone axes cleared about **465 m² (5,000 sq ft)
  of birch in 4 hours**, over 100 trees, with an original Neolithic axe head.
  [Bactra review of Budiansky](https://bactra.org/reviews/natures-keepers/stone-axes.html),
  [Jørgensen report record, tDAR](https://core.tdar.org/document/420577/tree-felling-with-original-neolithic-flint-axes-in-draved-wood-report-on-the-experiments-in-1952-54)
- Ratios from earlier work: Saraydar & Shimada (1971, 1973) put steel at **5.1:1 and 6.4:1** over
  a ground stone axe (one tree each, measured by depth of cut). Mathieu & Meyer (1997) found metal
  axes "markedly faster" than stone, especially on larger trees, and **bronze about as good as
  steel**. [Penn Museum, Expedition](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/a-tree-falls-in-philadelphia/),
  [tDAR: Mathieu & Meyer 1997](https://core.tdar.org/document/413933/comparing-axe-heads-of-stone-bronze-and-steel-studies-in-experimental-archaeology),
  [Cambridge: Saraydar & Shimada](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/quantitative-comparison-of-efficiency-between-a-stone-axe-and-a-steel-axe/2CBE5DA3F7FDC78E404EF6885B9B7528)
- Summing up: on small trees stone is close to metal; on trunks over about 15 cm metal pulls far
  ahead. A fair game ratio is **stone 3-5x slower than steel**, worse on big hardwood.
- Stone is blunter than steel: strike at more than 45° or the blade glances off; a celt works more
  like a wedge. [TheTVDB, Primitive Technology "Stone Axe (celt)"](https://www.thetvdb.com/series/primitive-technology/episodes/5727015)
- Copper (Ötzi replica): a yew felled in about **35-45 minutes**, resharpened once or not at all
  (teaching guide and blog reports, original papers not checked).
  [Ursula Frayne library guide](https://ursulafrayne.libguides.com/year7/humanities/investigatingtheancientpast/otzitheiceman),
  [TYWKIWDBI blog](https://tywkiwdbi.blogspot.com/2015/02/otzis-copper-axe.html)

## 3. Hafting: putting a handle on

**When.**
- Kathu Pan, South Africa: stone points about **500,000 years** old with impact damage and base
  thinning for a haft, the earliest hafted spear tips.
  [University of Toronto](https://utoronto.ca/news/archaeologists-identify-spear-tips-used-hunting-half-million-years-ago),
  [PubMed: Wilkins et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23161998/)
- Campitello, Italy: birch-bark tar on two flint flakes, at least **~191-200 ka**, the oldest known
  glue. [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6586293),
  [Kozowyk et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5579016)
- Sibudu, South Africa, about **70 ka**: glue mixed from acacia gum and red ochre (sometimes
  beeswax), heated by a fire; ochre makes the gum stick better.
  [Wikipedia: Sibudu Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibudu_Cave),
  [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/7893-evidence-stone-age-multitasking.html)

**Methods.**
- Split or forked stick (wedge the stone in the split), a socket or hole burned or chiselled
  through the handle, then lashing (sinew, rawhide, plant cord) and/or glue (birch tar, pine resin
  mixed with charcoal or ochre, plant gum).
- Ötzi's copper axe (about 3300 BCE): 99.7% copper blade, set into a forked **yew** haft about 60 cm
  long with **birch tar**, bound tight with **leather straps**. His flint dagger: blade forced into an
  ash handle and bound with **sinew**. His arrowheads: bound with **plant fibre**; fletching glued with
  birch tar. [South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology](https://www.iceman.it/en/oetzi/equipment)
- Plant's celt: the handle was shaped with a stone chisel and log mallet, and fire was used to
  harden the wood and burn out the hole. [TheTVDB episode](https://www.thetvdb.com/series/primitive-technology/episodes/5727015)
  His stone adze head was lashed to an L-shaped handle with lawyer cane.
  [TheTVDB episode](https://www.thetvdb.com/series/primitive-technology/episodes/5727014)

**How long.**
- Plant's celt: **handle a day and a morning**; **head about a week and a day** (a large basalt
  piece, pecked and ground). [TheTVDB episode](https://www.thetvdb.com/series/primitive-technology/episodes/5727015)
- Dickson, an Australian experimenter, made a hafted edge-ground axe in **about 5 hours** from start
  to finish, picking river cobbles already near axe shape.
  [Stone Tools Museum](https://stonetoolsmuseum.com/artefact/australia/modern-art-f-p-dickson/6289/)
- No study gives a clean clock time for lashing a spear point. Hafts must be found, shaped and
  prepared, and this costs more time than the stone insert.
  [Archaeology Southwest](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/2016/09/07/hands-on-archaeology-how-to-haft-a-point-into-a-foreshaft)
- Birch tar: the simplest method (bark rolled in hot ash) gives about **1 g of tar per 100 g of
  bark**, enough to haft one small tool. A covered earth dome kept burning for hours gave up to
  **9.6 g per 100 g** (15.7 g in one run).
  [Kozowyk et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5579016)
- Tar from about 1 hour of above-ground heating was as strong as tar from 20 hours underground.
  [Koch & Schmidt 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8748610)

**Why lashing alone loosens.** Sinew and rawhide go on wet and shrink tight as they dry; wet again,
they swell and slacken, and a sharp stone edge can cut them.
[Archaeology Southwest](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/2016/09/07/hands-on-archaeology-how-to-haft-a-point-into-a-foreshaft),
[Elfshot blog](https://elfshotgallery.blogspot.com/2010/01/sinew-and-ergonomic-stone-tools.html)
Glue alone is brittle; the two together (Ötzi's axe) hold best.

## 4. Cordage

- Oldest direct find: a **3-ply cord** from Abri du Maras, France, **41-52 ka**, twisted from the
  inner bark of a tree (probably conifer), found wrapped on a 60 mm stone tool. Neanderthals made it.
  [PubMed: Hardy et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32273518/),
  [The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/image-of-the-day-ancient-fiber-technology-67409)
- Fibres: nettle, dogbane, milkweed, flax, yucca, lime (linden) and willow inner bark, grasses,
  cattail; animal sinew and rawhide. Ötzi's kit used lime bast to sew, grass to mend clothes, plant
  fibre to bind arrowheads and animal fibre for a 2 m bowstring.
  [South Tyrol Museum](https://www.iceman.it/en/oetzi/equipment)
- Method: reverse-wrap (twist each strand one way, wrap the two strands round each other the other
  way, so they lock).
- Speed: about **10 feet (3 m) per hour** with practice (Norm Kidder, quoted in a California State
  Parks handbook); a hobbyist reports about **4 feet (1.2 m) per hour** with dogbane. So **1 m of 2-ply
  cord takes about 20-50 minutes**, not counting getting the fibre.
  [California State Parks ELP handbook (PDF)](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/470/files/ELP%20Handbook%20Addendum.pdf),
  [BladeForums](https://www.bladeforums.com/threads/cordage-and-the-reverse-twist.502683/)
- Fibre prep can take far longer than twisting. Lime bark must rot ("ret") in water to free the
  bast: **several weeks to months**, 6-8 weeks typical, 53 days in one test; fresh water works
  better than sea water. Lime bast rope is lighter than hemp, but hemp holds about 3x the load.
  [Viking Ship Museum, Roskilde](https://vikingeskibsmuseet.dk/en/news/lime-bast-rope-for-the-gislinge-boats-rig-1),
  [Rockingham Forest Trust](https://www.rockinghamforest.org.uk/post/rope-from-trees)
- Bast is best harvested early spring to early summer.
  [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/archaeology/neanderthal-cords-08317.html)
- Plant's first hut was tied with vine (lawyer cane), not made cord.
  [Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/2017/11/24/new-area-starting-from-scratch/)

## 5. Sticks: digging sticks, spears, throwing sticks

- Kalambo Falls, Zambia: two logs shaped and joined with stone tools, at least **476,000 years**
  old, the oldest wooden structure. Wooden tools there are 324-390 ka.
  [Aberystwyth University](https://aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2023/09/title-266728-en.html)
- Clacton spear tip, England: **yew**, about **400 ka**, the oldest worked wooden weapon.
  [Wikipedia: Clacton Spear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clacton_Spear)
- Schöningen spears, Germany: long thought ~300 ka, redated in 2025 to about **200 ka**
  (so likely Neanderthal; not every expert agrees).
  [University of York](https://www.york.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/2025/research/age-schoningen-spears)
- Poggetti Vecchi, Italy, about **171 ka**: 58 **boxwood** sticks over 1 m long, rounded at one end and
  pointed at the other, likely digging sticks. They were charred on purpose, then scraped with
  stone: fire was a step in making them. Experimenters could not shape boxwood without charring it
  first. [PNAS, Aranguren et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5834685)
- Digging sticks dig roots and tubers, burrowing animals and ant nests, and work soil; points are
  sometimes hardened in fire. New Guinea sticks: about 2 m by 4 cm, and 1 m by 2 cm.
  [Wikipedia: Digging stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digging_stick)
- Deadfall vs cut: early people had no tool for felling. A spear or digging stick is a straight
  sapling or branch, cut at its thin base with a flake or hand axe, or broken, then scraped and
  fire-hardened. Firewood came from dead wood on the ground. (Our reading of the evidence above.)

## 6. Fire

**Using it vs making it.**
- Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa: burned bone and ash about **1 Ma**, maybe 1.07-1.79 Ma, but
  probably fire carried in from wildfires, not lit at will.
  [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ashes-oldest-controlled-fire),
  [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/archaeology/wonderwerk-cave-fire-use-14848.html)
- Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel, **780 ka**: repeated hearths, and fish cooked there.
  [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-11-oldest-evidence-cook-food.pdf)
- Barnham, England, about **400 ka**: the earliest evidence of **making** fire: heated ground,
  fire-cracked hand axes and two pieces of **pyrite** carried in from elsewhere (pyrite sparks when
  struck with flint). [UCL News, Dec 2025](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/dec/british-discovery-shows-humans-made-fire-350000-earlier-thought)
- Late Neanderthals (~50 ka, France) used hand axes as strike-a-lights against pyrite (wear marks
  on 20 bifaces). [Phys.org: Sorensen et al. 2018](https://m.phys.org/news/2018-07-neanderthals.html)
- Ötzi carried tinder fungus (*Fomes fomentarius*), flint and pyrite.
  [Wikipedia: Fire making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_making),
  [Wikipedia: Amadou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amadou)

**Methods.** Hand drill (a stick spun between the palms in a notch in a board; the most common
method worldwide; needs no cord), bow drill (a cord-and-bow spins a short spindle; easier on the
hands), fire plough (rub a stick along a groove), fire saw, and percussion: **flint struck on pyrite
or marcasite** (flint on flint does not spark; flint on steel does, but that is the Iron Age).
[Wikipedia: Fire making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_making)

**Tinder.** Dry grass, shredded inner bark, birch bark, palm fibre, rotten "punk" wood, tinder
fungus (amadou) to catch sparks. Friction drills make their own hot dust, which forms the ember.
[Wikipedia: Fire making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_making),
[Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/2017/11/24/new-area-starting-from-scratch/)

**How long.**
- Bow drill with good materials: an ember in **well under a minute**; soft stalks (goldenrod,
  mullein, teasel) in seconds. Faster bowing is what matters, not longer strokes. The ember forms
  at about **370 °C**. [University of Dayton Magazine](https://udayton.edu/magazine/2023/12/lighting-fire.php)
- Hand drill, tested over **370** wood pairs: an easy pair takes about a minute; average effort
  about 3-4 minutes; many fail. Hand drill is rated harder than bow drill (average effort 4.0 vs 3.2).
  Without a notch, an ember came only 28% of the time. Light, soft spindles work best.
  [Primitive Ways: hand drill study](https://www.primitiveways.com/hand_drill.html)
- Novices: no controlled study found. Forum accounts (Bushcraft UK, Permies; not checked in full)
  describe lots of smoke and black dust but no ember at first, and one hobbyist needing about 25
  embers before feeling reliable.
  [Permies thread](https://permies.com/t/38796/Friction-fire-making-troubleshooting)
- A fair game ratio: first friction fire for a beginner is **tens of minutes to hours** of attempts,
  plus time to find a dry, soft spindle and board and dry tinder; with skill, **1-4 minutes**.

**What fire gave:** warmth and light, cooking (fish at 780 ka), protection, shaping and hardening
wood (Poggetti Vecchi), birch tar for glue, heat-treating stone, and later firing clay and smelting.
Plant lit his first fire in a new area mainly to drive off mosquitoes.
[Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/2017/11/24/new-area-starting-from-scratch/)

## 7. What was gathered from the ground

- **Stone:** rounded river and beach cobbles; flint nodules eroding out of chalk, lying in river
  gravels, on beaches and on slopes. Scots in flint-poor areas always gathered flint as beach
  pebbles; flint nodules up to 10-12 lb lay on the bed of the River Trent at low water. People
  usually did not quarry at first; they picked up.
  [Society of Antiquaries of Scotland (PDF)](https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/sair/article/download/10448/10527/10829),
  [Nottinghamshire HER](https://her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MNT5792),
  [Schick & Toth (PDF)](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/pdfs/oldowan-ch1-schick-toth.pdf)
- **Wood:** dead branches for fire; green saplings for spears and digging sticks (sections 5, 6).
- **Fibre and tinder:** dry grass, bark, palm fibre, vines, nettle and other fibre plants, fungus
  (sections 4, 6).
- **Food:** shellfish at low tide (mussels, periwinkles, limpets) from about **164 ka** at Pinnacle
  Point, South Africa. [Nature, Marean et al. 2007](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature06204)
  Roots and tubers by digging stick; berries, nuts, honey.
- **Carcasses:** Oldowan bones show cut marks from skinning and defleshing, and hammerstone marks
  from breaking long bones for marrow. Some of this was scavenging.
  [Schick & Toth (PDF)](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/pdfs/oldowan-ch1-schick-toth.pdf)
- **Bone:** limb bones of elephant and hippo, knapped like stone (section 9).

## 8. What survival teaching does first, and what John Plant did

**Rule of threes:** about **3 minutes** without air, **3 hours** without shelter in harsh weather,
**3 days** without water, **3 weeks** without food. So: shelter, water, fire, food, in that order;
food is rarely urgent. These are rough averages, not clocks.
[Wikipedia: Rule of threes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_threes_(survival)),
[Trail Hiking Australia](https://www.trailhiking.com.au/safety/survival-rule-of-threes-and-survival-priorities/)

**John Plant (Primitive Technology), "starting from scratch" in a new area, in order:**
1. Gathered fire-stick wood (*Abroma mollis*), knapped a small stone blade to strip it, and
   collected palm-fibre tinder, kept dry under a palm leaf.
2. Made a simple hand axe from a creek stone; cut a staff; cleared a path and a hut circle.
3. Cut 8 saplings 2.75 m long, set them 25 cm deep in a 2.5 m circle, tied the tops with vine.
4. Cut about **600** palm fronds with a stone flake and thatched a dome hut.
5. Lit a fire by **hand drill** and brought it into the hut, with a stone hearth.
6. Capped the roof, built a raised bed.
[Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/2017/11/24/new-area-starting-from-scratch/)

**His timings:**
- Ground celt axe: head **about 8 days** (big basalt, pecked with a quartz hammerstone, ground,
  polished); handle **a day and a morning**.
  [TheTVDB episode](https://www.thetvdb.com/series/primitive-technology/episodes/5727015)
- Later steps for scale: firing lime about 1.5 h; a pot boiling over rocks about 30 min; a
  wood-fired furnace built in about a week (mostly drying); a tiled hut 102 days.
  [Primitive Technology blog](https://primitivetechnology.wordpress.com/),
  [GearJunkie](https://gearjunkie.com/outdoor/axe-kiln-tiled-hut-natural)

**Ground (polished) axes vs knapped ones.**
- Grinding a 22 cm flint axe blank by hand took **5 h 30 min**; a 24 cm one **4 h 30 min** (Madsen's
  Danish experiments). Flint takes about twice as long to grind as diorite. A popular account puts
  a fully smooth flint axe at about **60 hours**.
  [Journal of Danish Archaeology: Madsen](https://tidsskrift.dk/jda/article/view/126138),
  [The Past](https://the-past.com/feature/testing-times-examining-insights-from-experimental-archaeology)
- Pecking a North American grooved axe: just under **40 hours**.
  [Lithic Studies: grooved-axe replication](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/lithicstudies/article/view/1887)
- So: knapped flint hand axe in **minutes** (expert); ground axe in **hours to days**; tough stone
  (basalt) is pecked, flint is flaked then ground.

## 9. Bone and antler, and the order of materials

- **Wood and stone:** stone flakes by 2.6 Ma (wood rarely survives, but Kalambo Falls shows
  shaped wood at 476 ka).
- **Bone:** systematic knapped bone tools at Olduvai about **1.5 Ma** (27 pieces, mostly elephant
  and hippo limb bones), over a million years earlier than thought.
  [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/1-5-million-year-old-bone-tools-crafted-by-human-ancestors-in-tanzania-are-oldest-of-their-kind)
  At Boxgrove, England (~480 ka), an elephant-bone hammer was used to retouch flint hand axes;
  bone and antler soft hammers allowed finer knapping.
  [Natural History Museum, 2026](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2026/january/ancient-humans-made-elephant-bone-tools-europe-half-a-million-years-ago.html)
  Ötzi's retoucher: a lime-wood stick with an antler tip.
  [South Tyrol Museum](https://www.iceman.it/en/oetzi/equipment)
- **Copper:** native copper cold-hammered (and annealed) at Çayönü, Turkey, about **7200-6600 BCE**;
  first smelting at Belovode, Serbia, about **5000 BCE**.
  [WorldAtlas](https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/what-was-the-first-metal-used-by-man.html),
  [Radivojević et al., UCL (PDF)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10137845/1/0350-02411464007R.pdf)
- **Bronze:** Near East about **3300-1200 BCE**; harder than copper.
  [Wikipedia: Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age)
- **Iron:** common from about **1200 BCE** (needs higher heat, harder to work).
  [Wikipedia: Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age)
- Order: wood/stone -> bone/antler -> native copper (cold-hammered) -> smelted copper -> bronze ->
  iron. Bronze axes fell about as fast as steel ones (section 2).

## What this suggests for a game

- **Put on the ground:** loose stones (rounded river/beach cobbles, flint nodules near chalk, in
  gravel and on beaches), dead sticks and branches, dry grass, bark, vines, fibre plants, fungus on
  dead trees, berries, nuts, shellfish on shores, the odd carcass and bones. Everything in the first
  hour is picked up, not cut.
- **First step: break two stones together.** A sharp flake is the first tool: seconds, but a novice
  gets a usable one only about every 2-3 strikes, and can cut a finger. Good stone (flint, chert,
  obsidian) knaps; granite and sandstone don't (sandstone becomes the grinding slab).
- **A flake cuts, it does not chop.** It skins, butchers, cuts grass, fronds, bark and thin
  saplings. Hand-held stone cannot fell a real tree; it can cut a staff or a sapling.
- **A hand axe is a skill, not a recipe.** Experts make one in 3-15 minutes; novices failed after
  90 hours of training. A skill or chance-of-failure gate fits here better than a plain recipe.
- **Fire is earned, not instant.** Friction fire needs the right dry, soft wood plus tinder and can
  fail for a beginner many times; a skilled hand drill takes 1-4 minutes. Flint plus pyrite is a
  fast, rare alternative (pyrite is a find, not common).
- **Fire unlocks things:** hardening a digging stick or spear point, birch tar, cooking, warmth and
  light at night, keeping animals and insects away.
- **Cord is slow:** about 20-50 minutes per metre by hand, and bast fibre can need weeks of soaking.
  Vines and green withies are the quick early binding; sinew and rawhide come after a kill.
- **A hafted axe is a project, not a craft click.** Roughly: flake = seconds; knapped hand axe =
  minutes; handle = hours to a day; ground celt head = 5 hours to several days; full hafted axe =
  a day or more. Lashing alone loosens when wet; tar or resin plus lashing holds.
- **Felling ratios:** on thin saplings a stone axe needs about 8x the strokes of steel; on
  medium trees stone is about 3-5x slower than steel; softwood (willow) about twice as fast as
  hardwood (ash) of the same size. A 20 cm willow is 30-60 min with a stone axe.
- **Metal tiers:** copper and bronze close most of the gap; bronze is about as good as steel for
  felling. Iron is better for hardness and toughness, not much faster at chopping.
- **Natural order of a first day:** gather stones, sticks and tinder -> knap flakes -> cut a staff
  and fronds/saplings -> rough shelter -> fire (if materials are dry) -> food from the ground
  (berries, nuts, shellfish, roots). The axe, cord and spear come on day 2 and later.
- **Bone comes early, not late:** knapped bone tools are 1.5 million years old; bone and antler
  are good hammers for finer knapping, so a carcass is also a tool source.
